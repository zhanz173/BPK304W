1 Spearman's Rank Correlation \((\rho)\)

Spearman's \(\rho\) measures the strength and direction of a monotonic relationship between two variables. A monotonic relationship simply means that as one variable increases, the other tends to consistently increase (or decrease) — even if the relationship is not a perfect straight line.

When to Use Spearman Instead of Pearson

Ordinal Data
Data consists of ranked categories (e.g., pain scales, satisfaction surveys, athletic rankings) rather than measured continuous values.
Skewed Distributions
One or both variables are severely non-normal (strongly skewed, heavy-tailed, or containing large outliers that distort the Pearson calculation).
Monotonic, Not Linear
The relationship is consistently directional but curves (e.g., drug dose and effect — increasing dose raises effect, but the rate of change slows). Pearson misses this.

Step-by-Step Mechanics

1
Rank Each Variable
Assign a rank (1 to \(n\)) to each variable independently. Rank 1 = smallest value. For ties, assign the average of the ranks they would have occupied (e.g., if two values tie for ranks 3 and 4, both receive rank 3.5).
2
Calculate Differences \(d_i\)
For each observation, subtract the rank of \(Y\) from the rank of \(X\): \[d_i = \text{Rank}(X_i) - \text{Rank}(Y_i)\]
3
Apply the Formula
Square each difference, sum them all, and apply: \[\rho = 1 - \frac{6\sum d_i^2}{n(n^2-1)}\] This simplification holds exactly when there are no tied ranks.

Worked Example: Sleep Quality and Reaction-Time Performance

Six participants were rated on average nightly sleep hours and a standardised reaction-time performance score (higher = better). Both variables are slightly skewed in the full sample, so Spearman's \(\rho\) is preferred over Pearson's \(r\).

Handling Tied Ranks

When ties exist (e.g., two participants both score 55), assign the mean of the tied ranks. If two observations tie for ranks 3 and 4, both receive rank 3.5. The simplified formula above loses a small amount of accuracy when there are many ties; in that case, use the full Pearson formula applied to the ranks instead of the raw values.

Key Distinction — Monotonic vs. Linear
Pearson's \(r\) demands a linear relationship: equal absolute changes in \(X\) must correspond to equal changes in \(Y\). Spearman's \(\rho\) only requires a monotonic relationship: as \(X\) increases, \(Y\) must tend to increase (or decrease), without requiring constant rates of change. A perfectly curved exponential relationship can have \(\rho = 1\) but \(r < 1\).
Common Misconception
The formula squares the rank differences, so the resulting \(\rho\) is not always positive. When high ranks of \(X\) consistently pair with low ranks of \(Y\), the large \(d_i^2\) values can make \(\frac{6\sum d_i^2}{n(n^2-1)} > 1\), yielding a negative \(\rho\) — correctly indicating a strong negative monotonic relationship.

2 Chi-Squared Goodness-of-Fit Test

The Chi-Squared (\(\chi^2\)) Goodness-of-Fit test answers: "Does our observed sample distribution match an expected theoretical distribution?" It applies when you have a single categorical variable and want to test whether each category occurs as frequently as a theory predicts.

The Null Hypothesis

The null hypothesis specifies the expected proportion \(p_k\) for each category \(k\). The most common version assumes a uniform distribution (all categories equally likely), but any theoretically motivated distribution can be used.

$$H_0: p_1 = p_2 = \cdots = p_k = \frac{1}{k} \quad \text{(uniform case)}$$

Expected Frequencies

For each category, calculate the expected count if the null hypothesis were true:

$$E_i = N \times p_i$$

For the uniform case with \(k\) categories and \(N\) total observations, this simplifies to \(E_i = N/k\) for all \(i\).

The Test Statistic

The \(\chi^2\) statistic measures the total discrepancy between observed and expected counts. It squares the differences so that positive and negative deviations both contribute positively, then scales by the expected count so that absolute differences are comparable across categories:

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}$$

Degrees of Freedom and the Rule of 5

df
Degrees of Freedom
For Goodness-of-Fit with \(k\) categories: \[df = k - 1\] We lose one degree of freedom because once you know the counts in \(k-1\) categories and the total \(N\), the last category is fully determined.
E≥5
The Rule of 5
The \(\chi^2\) approximation is reliable only when every expected cell count \(E_i \ge 5\). If some cells have very small expected counts, consider combining adjacent categories or using an exact test (e.g., Fisher's exact test).

Misconception: Chi-Squared Is Always Positive
Because the formula squares \((O - E)\), every cell contributes a non-negative value. Therefore \(\chi^2 \ge 0\) always. A very small \(\chi^2\) (near 0) means observed counts are very close to expected — there is little evidence against \(H_0\).

3 Chi-Squared Test of Independence

The Test of Independence asks: "Are two categorical variables associated, or are they statistically independent?" The data are arranged in a contingency table cross-tabulating the two variables.

Structure of a Contingency Table

For two categorical variables with \(r\) row categories and \(c\) column categories, observations fall into an \(r \times c\) grid. The row totals (\(R_i\)), column totals (\(C_j\)), and grand total (\(N\)) determine the expected counts.

Expected Frequencies Under Independence

If the two variables are truly independent, knowing an observation's row category gives no information about its column category. Under this null, the expected count in cell \((i, j)\) is:

$$E_{ij} = \frac{R_i \times C_j}{N}$$

Intuitively: if row \(i\) contains a fraction \(R_i/N\) of all observations, and column \(j\) contains a fraction \(C_j/N\) of all observations, then — under independence — the fraction expected in cell \((i,j)\) is their product.

Test Statistic and Degrees of Freedom

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i,j} \frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}} \qquad df = (r-1)(c-1)$$
Why (r−1)(c−1)?
Once you know the row and column totals, how many cells can vary freely? If you fill in \((r-1)\) rows and \((c-1)\) columns, all remaining cells are automatically determined by the marginal totals. The number of freely-varying cells is \((r-1)(c-1)\).

The chi-squared distribution is a continuous approximation to what is actually a discrete count process. This approximation breaks down when expected cell counts are very small. As a rule of thumb: all \(E_{ij} \ge 5\). If not, merge categories or use Fisher's exact test for 2×2 tables.

4 Logistic Regression

Logistic regression models the probability of a binary outcome (\(Y \in \{0,1\}\)) as a function of one or more predictors. It is the standard tool when the dependent variable is categorical — pass/fail, healed/not healed, defaulted/paid.

4.1 Why OLS Fails for Binary Outcomes

When we force an ordinary linear regression model (\(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \varepsilon\)) onto binary data, we create a Linear Probability Model (LPM). This causes three structural problems:

!
Nonsensical Predictions
The line extends to \(-\infty\) and \(+\infty\), so predicted "probabilities" can be below 0 or above 1. A student with 0 study hours might be predicted to have a \(-20\%\) chance of passing — mathematically absurd.
~
Heteroscedasticity
For binary \(Y\), the variance of the error depends on \(X\): \(\text{Var}(Y|X) = p(1-p)\), which changes as \(p\) changes. This violates the constant-variance assumption that OLS requires for valid inference.
≠
Non-Normal Residuals
Because \(Y\) can only take values 0 or 1, residuals can only take two values for any given \(X\). They cannot be normally distributed, which invalidates standard OLS t-tests and confidence intervals.

4.2 The Logit Link: From Probability to a Linear Scale

To fix these issues, we transform probability into a quantity that can range from \(-\infty\) to \(+\infty\) — three conceptual steps:

1
Probability \(p\)
Range: \([0, 1]\)
\(p = P(Y=1|X)\)
→
2
Odds
Range: \([0, +\infty)\)
\(\text{Odds} = \dfrac{p}{1-p}\)
→
3
Log-Odds (Logit)
Range: \((-\infty, +\infty)\)
\(\ln\!\left(\dfrac{p}{1-p}\right)\)

By setting the log-odds equal to a linear model, we get a framework whose inputs are unbounded but whose outputs always map to valid probabilities:

$$\underbrace{\ln\!\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)}_{\text{logit}(p)} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X$$
Intuition — The Odds Example
If the probability of passing an exam is \(p = 0.80\), the odds are \(\frac{0.80}{0.20} = 4\) (read: "4 to 1 odds"). The log-odds are \(\ln(4) \approx 1.39\). This transformation is symmetric around 0: \(p = 0.50\) gives log-odds = 0; \(p > 0.50\) gives positive log-odds; \(p < 0.50\) gives negative log-odds.

4.3 The Logistic (Sigmoid) Function

Solving the logit equation for \(p\) gives the logistic function, which maps any linear combination of predictors back into a valid probability:

$$p = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 X)}}$$

This produces the classic S-shaped (sigmoid) curve. No matter how large or small \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 X\) becomes, the predicted probability always stays within \([0, 1]\) and approaches the bounds asymptotically — never crossing them.

At the midpoint
When \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 X = 0\), \(p = 0.5\). This is the decision boundary.
Left tail
As \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 X \to -\infty\), \(p \to 0\).
Right tail
As \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 X \to +\infty\), \(p \to 1\).

4.4 Estimation via Maximum Likelihood (MLE)

OLS minimises the Sum of Squared Errors. Logistic regression instead uses Maximum Likelihood Estimation: it finds the \(\beta\) values that make the observed pattern of 0s and 1s as probable as possible.

$$\ln L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[y_i \ln(p_i) + (1-y_i)\ln(1-p_i)\right]$$

Where \(p_i = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i)}}\). Because there is no closed-form solution, MLE is solved iteratively using algorithms like Newton-Raphson or gradient ascent. Modern software handles this automatically.

MLE Intuition
Imagine you are trying to position the S-curve so that it assigns high probability to each observed 1 and low probability to each observed 0. MLE finds the exact curve position that makes the entire observed dataset most likely to have occurred.

4.5 Interpreting Coefficients — Three Levels

A logistic regression coefficient \(\beta_1\) can be interpreted at three levels, each progressively more intuitive but requiring more computation:

Level Metric Interpretation Principle Example (\(\beta_1 = 0.40\))
Level 1 Log-Odds \((\beta_1)\) For each 1-unit increase in \(X\), the log-odds of the outcome increase by \(\beta_1\). "Each extra study hour increases log-odds of passing by 0.40." — Mathematically precise, but unintuitive.
Level 2 Odds Ratio \((e^{\beta_1})\) Exponentiating gives the multiplicative factor by which the odds change per 1-unit increase. \(e^{0.40} \approx 1.49\). "Each extra hour multiplies the odds of passing by 1.49 — a 49% increase in odds."
Level 3 Probability change The change in \(p\) is non-linear and depends on the starting value of \(X\). Plug specific values into the full equation. Going from 4 to 5 hours raises \(p\) from 0.31 to 0.42 (+11%). Going from 7 to 8 hours raises \(p\) from 0.74 to 0.82 (+8%). The effect tapers near the extremes.
Critical Distinction: Odds Ratio ≠ Relative Risk
Students frequently confuse the Odds Ratio with the Relative Risk (ratio of probabilities). Odds Ratio: "Your ratio of success-to-failure is 1.49× as high." Relative Risk: "You are 1.49× as likely to succeed." These are only approximately equal when the baseline event is rare (the "Rare Disease Assumption," roughly \(p_0 < 10\%\)). For common events, the Odds Ratio always overstates how much more likely the event is.

4.6 Evaluating Model Fit

Because logistic regression has no total sum of squares to partition, we cannot compute a standard \(R^2\). Instead, we use:

McFadden's Pseudo-\(R^2\)
\(R^2_{\text{McF}} = 1 - \dfrac{\ln L_{\text{full}}}{\ln L_{\text{null}}}\)
Compares the log-likelihood of the fitted model to an intercept-only (null) model. Values between 0.2 and 0.4 are considered excellent — this is a much lower numeric threshold than ordinary \(R^2\), so don't interpret it the same way.
Deviance
\(D = -2\ln L\)
The logistic analogue of residual sum of squares. Adding a predictor reduces deviance. The likelihood ratio test uses the difference in deviance between two nested models, which follows a \(\chi^2\) distribution under \(H_0\).
Confusion Matrix
Predicted vs. Actual
By applying a decision threshold (usually \(p \ge 0.5 \Rightarrow \hat{Y} = 1\)), we cross-tabulate predictions against true outcomes. Key metrics: Sensitivity (true positive rate) and Specificity (true negative rate). The optimal threshold depends on the relative cost of each error type.
