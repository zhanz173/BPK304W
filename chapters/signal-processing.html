1 What Is a Biological Signal?

A signal is a measurement that changes over time or space. In biomedical physiology and kinesiology, the changing quantity may be electrical activity, force, pressure, motion, temperature, flow, or joint angle.

The original physiological event is usually analog: it exists continuously in time and can take any value within a physical range. A computer cannot store that continuous event directly. It stores a finite list of numbers measured at specific instants.

3 Analog Signal Conditioning

The signal leaving a sensor is often tiny, noisy, shifted away from zero, or mismatched to the input range of the data acquisition board. Analog conditioning prepares the voltage before digitization.

Amplification

Small signals such as EEG and EMG may need gain before entering the A/D converter. Amplification helps the signal use more of the available input range.

Offset Removal

A constant voltage bias can push the recording away from zero. Removing the offset keeps the useful changes centered in the measurable range.

Analog Filtering

Low-pass, high-pass, or band-pass filters can reduce unwanted frequencies before the data become digital. The anti-aliasing filter is the most important example.

Isolation

Isolation protects the participant and equipment by preventing unsafe electrical pathways during biological recordings.

Why Amplification Matters
If a board accepts -10 V to +10 V but an EMG signal only spans -0.005 V to +0.005 V, most of the available digital codes are wasted on voltages the signal never reaches. Appropriate gain improves practical resolution.

4 Analog-to-Digital Conversion

An analog-to-digital converter, or ADC, turns a continuous voltage into a digital sequence. It performs two separate operations:

1
Sampling
Choose the instants in time when the voltage will be measured.
2
Quantization
Round each measured voltage to the nearest available digital level.
$$\text{Analog voltage } x(t) \rightarrow \text{digital samples } x[0], x[1], x[2], \ldots, x[n]$$

The square brackets remind us that a digital signal is indexed by sample number. To convert sample number back into time, we need the sampling rate.

Bit Depth and Voltage Resolution

$$\text{Number of levels} = 2^N \qquad \text{Voltage step} \approx \frac{V_{\max} - V_{\min}}{2^N - 1}$$

A 16-bit ADC has 65,536 levels. A 24-bit ADC has 16,777,216 levels. Higher bit depth reduces quantization error, but only if the rest of the acquisition system is quiet and well matched to the signal.

6 Sampling, Nyquist Frequency, and Aliasing

Sampling records the signal value at regular intervals. If samples are taken every \(h\) seconds, the sampling frequency is:

$$f_s = \frac{1}{h}$$

The Nyquist frequency is half the sampling rate:

$$f_N = \frac{f_s}{2}$$

To represent a signal component without ambiguity, the sampling rate must be greater than twice the highest frequency present in the signal:

$$f_s > 2f_{\max}$$
Aliasing Cannot Be Fixed Later
Aliasing occurs during sampling. Once a high-frequency component has folded into the stored digital data as a false lower frequency, digital filtering cannot reliably recover the original signal.

A Signal Is Rarely Just One Frequency

The Nyquist rule is written above as if a signal has a single frequency, but a physiological recording is really a mixture of many frequency components at once: a heartbeat's fundamental rhythm plus its harmonics, EMG energy spread across tens to hundreds of Hz, and higher-frequency noise from motion or electrical interference. \(f_{\max}\) means the highest frequency component with meaningful energy anywhere in that mixture, not the obvious repetition rate you can see by eye. That is precisely the question that frequency-domain analysis answers: next week's Fourier transform takes a digital signal and reports how much of it is made of each frequency, which is how \(f_{\max}\) gets determined in practice rather than guessed.

The Aliasing Formula

When \(f_s \le 2f_{\max}\), a sampled sine component does not disappear. It reappears at a lower, false frequency folded back into the range \(0\) to \(f_N\). For a single-frequency component sampled at rate \(f_s\), the apparent (aliased) frequency is:

$$f_{\text{alias}} = \left| f_{\text{signal}} - f_s \cdot \text{round}\!\left(\frac{f_{\text{signal}}}{f_s}\right) \right|$$

This is the same folding rule the interactive figure below uses to compute the "Apparent frequency" readout. Because aliasing folds each frequency component independently, a signal with many components can develop many false ones at once — another reason a proper analog anti-aliasing filter must run before sampling, not after.

8 Clipping, Saturation, and Dynamic Range

Clipping occurs when the signal exceeds the measurable range of the acquisition system. If the ADC accepts -10 V to +10 V, any true signal above +10 V will be stored as +10 V.

Clipping is often visible because peaks become flat. The problem is that the true peak amplitude is lost. No later processing step can know how high the clipped signal really was.

GAIN
Too Little Gain
The signal uses only a small part of the ADC range. Quantization steps may become visible or meaningful changes may be lost.
OK
Appropriate Gain
The signal uses much of the range while leaving enough margin for unexpectedly large values.
CLIP
Too Much Gain
Large peaks exceed the input range and saturate. The stored peak is no longer trustworthy.
Dynamic Range
Dynamic range is the span between the smallest and largest signals a system can measure usefully. A good setup amplifies enough to resolve small changes, but not so much that large events clip.

9 Check Your Understanding

Try answering each question before clicking it open. Work them in order — the later questions build on the sampling and aliasing ideas from Sections 6 and 7.

1. A goniometer signal is sampled at 200 Hz. What is the Nyquist frequency, and what does it mean for this recording?

The Nyquist frequency is \(f_s / 2 = 100\) Hz. Only components of the joint-angle signal below 100 Hz can be represented without ambiguity; any true signal content at or above 100 Hz will alias into a false lower frequency instead of being recorded correctly.

2. A 12 Hz sine component is sampled at 18 Hz. Using the aliasing formula from Section 6, what frequency will it appear as in the stored data?

\(f_{\text{alias}} = |12 - 18 \cdot \text{round}(12/18)| = |12 - 18 \cdot 1| = 6\) Hz. The true 12 Hz component is stored as a false 6 Hz wave, matching the example in the Section 7 demo. Note that 18 Hz also fails the Nyquist rule here, since \(f_N = 9\) Hz is less than 12 Hz.

3. Why must the anti-aliasing filter be an analog filter applied before the ADC, rather than a digital filter applied after?

Aliasing happens at the moment of sampling. Once a high-frequency component has folded into a false lower frequency, the stored samples cannot distinguish it from a real low-frequency component. No digital filter applied afterward can tell them apart, so the only way to prevent aliasing is to remove the offending high frequencies from the analog voltage before it is sampled.

4. Two setups record the same EMG signal: one with a 4-bit ADC, one with a 16-bit ADC, both using the same voltage range. Which produces a smaller voltage step per code, and why does that matter?

The 16-bit ADC has a much smaller step, since step size is approximately \((V_{\max}-V_{\min})/(2^N-1)\) and \(2^{16}-1\) is far larger than \(2^4-1\). A smaller step means the quantized signal follows the true analog voltage more closely, reducing quantization error, provided the rest of the acquisition chain (amplifier noise, electrode contact) is not the limiting factor first.

5. A researcher increases the ADC's input range from -5/+5 V to -10/+10 V without changing the amplifier gain on a small EMG signal. What happens to resolution, and why might this be a bad trade?

The voltage step gets larger, since the same number of quantization levels now has to cover twice the range, so the smaller EMG signal now uses a smaller fraction of the ADC's levels and quantization error increases. The wider range prevents clipping on large signals but wastes resolution on a signal that never approaches the new limits; matching gain to the true signal amplitude is usually the better fix.

6. How to choose sampling rate for biological signals

The sampling rate should be at least twice the highest frequency component of interest in the signal (Nyquist criterion). In practice, it is often chosen to be 5-10 times higher than the maximum frequency to ensure accurate representation and to allow for anti-aliasing filtering. For example, if the highest frequency of interest in an EMG signal is 500 Hz, a sampling rate of at least 1000 Hz is required, but 2000-5000 Hz may be used for better fidelity.
