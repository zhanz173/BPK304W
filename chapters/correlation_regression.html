Correlation & Regression

Two complementary tools for understanding relationships between variables: correlation quantifies association, while regression predicts and models the relationship.

1 Correlation vs. Regression

Both tools explore the relationship between variables, but they answer different questions.

CORR
Correlation
Measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship. It treats both variables equally: the correlation of X with Y is the same as the correlation of Y with X.
REGR
Regression
Predicts a dependent variable (Y) from one or more independent variables (X). It is directional: changing which variable is the outcome changes the model.
Warning: The Golden Rule
Correlation does not imply causation. Two variables can be highly correlated because both are influenced by a hidden third variable: a confounding variable. Always ask whether a plausible mechanism supports the statistical pattern.

2 Pearson's \(r\)

For linear relationships between two continuous variables, we use the Pearson Product-Moment Correlation Coefficient (\(r\)). It summarizes how closely two variables move together as a number between \(-1\) and \(+1\).

The Formula

Think of \(r\) as a standardized ratio: how much the variables vary together divided by the amount of variation each variable has on its own.

$$r = \frac{\sum (X - \bar{X})(Y - \bar{Y})}{\sqrt{\sum (X - \bar{X})^2 \cdot \sum (Y - \bar{Y})^2}}$$
  • Numerator: the cross-deviation sum, which is proportional to the covariance of \(X\) and \(Y\). Do high values of \(X\) tend to appear with high values of \(Y\)?
  • Denominator: the product of their individual variation. This normalizes the score to the range \([-1, +1]\).

Interpreting the Value

ValueMeaningExample
r = +1Perfect positive linear relationshipEvery increase in X corresponds to an exactly linear increase in Y.
r = -1Perfect negative linear relationshipAs study hours increase, error rate decreases perfectly.
r = 0No linear relationshipA curved relationship may still be present even when \(r\) is near zero.

Testing Whether a Correlation Is Statistically Significant

A sample correlation \(r\) estimates the population correlation \(\rho\). To test whether the observed relationship is stronger than expected from random sampling variation alone, the usual null hypothesis is:

$$H_0: \rho = 0 \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad H_A: \rho \ne 0$$

When the assumptions for Pearson correlation are reasonable, the test statistic is:

$$t = r\sqrt{\frac{n-2}{1-r^2}}, \qquad df = n - 2$$
Interpretation
A small p-value suggests that a population correlation of zero is not a good explanation for the observed data. It does not prove causation, and it does not tell you whether the relationship is practically important.
Quick check: If resting heart rate and aerobic fitness have \(r = -0.68\), what does the negative sign tell you? What does it not prove?

3 Assumptions & Pitfalls

Statistical Requirements

I/R
Continuous Scale
Both variables should be interval or ratio data, such as height, temperature, time, force, or test score. For ordinal rankings, use Spearman's rank correlation.
LIN
Linearity
Pearson's \(r\) captures only the linear component of a relationship. Always visualize the data with a scatter plot before interpreting \(r\).
OUT
No Extreme Outliers
Pearson's \(r\) is sensitive to extreme points. One high-leverage outlier can inflate a weak association or obscure a strong one.

Four Common Pitfalls

Pitfall A
The "Causation" Trap
A and B moving together does not mean A causes B. Both may be driven by a hidden variable C. Always ask what mechanism could explain the pattern.
Pitfall B
Anscombe's Quartet
Very different datasets can share the same means, variances, and \(r = 0.816\), while telling completely different visual stories. Always plot the data.
Pitfall C
Restriction of Range
Looking only at a narrow subset of the data range can make a real correlation vanish, shrink, or even reverse.
Pitfall D
The "Zero Means Nothing" Myth
\(r = 0\) means no linear relationship. A U-shape, curve, or threshold effect can still represent a strong non-linear relationship.

4 Regression Fundamentals

Regression fits an equation to data so we can predict an outcome and describe how that prediction changes as predictors change.

The Linear Equation

$$Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \epsilon$$
\(Y\)
Dependent Variable
The outcome you are predicting.
\(X\)
Independent Variable
The predictor used to estimate the outcome.
\(\beta_0\)
Y-Intercept
The expected value of \(Y\) when \(X = 0\). Interpret only when \(X = 0\) is meaningful.
\(\beta_1\)
Slope
The expected change in \(Y\) for a 1-unit increase in \(X\).
\(\epsilon\)
Error Term
The residual variation in \(Y\) that the model does not explain.

Example

Suppose we record weekly endurance training hours and estimated VO2 max for a group of students. A simple model might be:

$$\widehat{VO2max} = 36.2 + 2.1(\text{training hours per week})$$

The slope says that each additional weekly training hour is associated with an expected 2.1 ml/kg/min higher VO2 max. The model is predictive and associational; it does not prove that adding one hour of training would cause exactly that gain for every person.

Prediction vs. Explanation

Regression can be used for both prediction and explanation, but those goals are not identical. A model can predict well without revealing a causal mechanism, and a statistically significant predictor may still add little practical predictive value.

Extrapolation Warning
Regression predictions are most defensible within the observed range of the predictor. If the training-hours data range from 0 to 8 hours per week, using the line to predict VO2 max at 20 hours per week assumes the same linear pattern continues far beyond the data.

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)

The best-fitting line is found by minimizing the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE), also called the Residual Sum of Squares (RSS). We square residuals so positive and negative errors do not cancel out:

$$SSE = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (Y_i - \hat{Y}_i)^2$$

Calculating the Coefficients

The OLS solution has closed-form formulas. The slope is derived first, then the intercept ensures the line passes through \((\bar{X}, \bar{Y})\):

$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})(Y_i - \bar{Y})}{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2} \qquad \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{Y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{X}$$

Uncertainty in the Slope

The fitted slope \(\hat{\beta}_1\) is an estimate, not the exact population value. We usually report a standard error, confidence interval, and p-value for the slope:

$$\hat{\beta}_1 \pm t^* \cdot SE(\hat{\beta}_1)$$

A 95% confidence interval gives a range of plausible values for the true population slope. If the interval for \(\beta_1\) does not include 0, that corresponds to a statistically significant slope test at approximately \(\alpha = 0.05\) for a two-sided test.

Connecting Correlation and Regression: \(R^2\)

In simple linear regression, squaring Pearson's correlation gives the Coefficient of Determination:

$$R^2 = r^2$$
Interpretation
\(R^2\) is the proportion of variance in \(Y\) explained by \(X\). If \(r = 0.8\), then \(R^2 = 0.64\): 64% of the variability in the outcome is accounted for by the predictor.

5 Simple Linear Regression (SLR)

SLR models the relationship between one independent variable \(X\) and one dependent variable \(Y\) by fitting a straight line to observed data.

Core Assumptions: LINE

For valid statistical inference, four key assumptions should be checked:

L
Linearity
The relationship between \(X\) and \(Y\) should be linear. Check this with a scatter plot.
I
Independence
Residuals should be independent. Repeated measures and time-series data often violate this assumption.
N
Normality
Residuals should be approximately normally distributed, especially for small samples.
E
Equal Variance
Residual spread should be roughly constant across fitted values. A fan shape suggests heteroscedasticity.
How to Check
After fitting the model, examine a residuals vs. fitted values plot for linearity and equal variance, and a Q-Q plot of residuals for normality.

Reading Diagnostic Plots

PatternWhat It SuggestsPossible Response
Residuals curve upward or downwardThe relationship may not be linear.Consider a transformed predictor, polynomial term, or a nonlinear model.
Residual spread forms a fan shapeUnequal variance, also called heteroscedasticity.Consider a transformation, weighted regression, or robust standard errors.
Q-Q plot has strong tail departuresResiduals may be non-normal, especially in the tails.Check outliers and consider whether inference based on normal residuals is reliable.
One point sits far from the restThe point may be an outlier, high-leverage point, or influential observation.Investigate the data source and compare results with and without the point.

Outliers, Leverage, and Influence

Not all unusual points affect a regression line in the same way. An outlier has an unusual \(Y\) value, a high-leverage point has an unusual \(X\) value, and an influential point noticeably changes the fitted model if it is removed.

Diagnostic Tool
Cook's distance is commonly used to flag influential observations. A large Cook's distance is not automatic permission to delete a point; it is a prompt to investigate measurement error, data-entry issues, and whether the model is appropriate.

6 Multiple Linear Regression (MLR)

MLR expands SLR by using two or more independent variables to predict one dependent variable. This helps account for multiple factors at once.

The Model Equation

$$Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \cdots + \beta_p X_p + \epsilon$$
  • \(p\): the number of predictor terms in the model, not including the intercept.
  • \(\beta_1, \beta_2, \dots, \beta_p\): partial regression coefficients. Each coefficient estimates the expected change in \(Y\) per 1-unit change in that predictor, holding all other predictors constant.

Geometry

SLR fits a best-fit line (1-D). MLR fits a best-fit plane for two predictors or a hyperplane (n-D) for three or more predictors. The OLS principle still applies: choose the coefficients that minimize SSE.

Additional Assumption: No Severe Multicollinearity

Warning: Multicollinearity
In MLR, predictors can be correlated, but severe overlap makes coefficient estimates unstable and hard to interpret. When predictors overlap too much, the model struggles to isolate each predictor's individual association with \(Y\). Detect this with the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF); VIF > 10 is a common warning sign, and some fields use lower cutoffs such as 5.

7 Evaluating Model Fit

To judge model fit, compare how much variation the model explains with how much unexplained error remains.

A. Sum of Squared Errors (SSE)

SSE, also called Residual Sum of Squares (RSS), measures total unexplained variation. It sums squared distances between observed and predicted values:

$$SSE = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (Y_i - \hat{Y}_i)^2$$

A lower SSE means predictions are closer to the observed data. OLS works by minimizing this quantity.

Limitation
SSE is scale-dependent. If you convert metres to millimetres, SSE becomes much larger even though the model's fit has not changed.

B. Coefficient of Determination (\(R^2\))

To create a scale-free metric, compare SSE with the Total Sum of Squares (SST), the total variation in \(Y\) around its mean:

$$SST = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i - \bar{Y})^2 \qquad R^2 = 1 - \frac{SSE}{SST}$$

The variance partition is:

SSR (Explained)
SSE (Residual)

SST (Total) = SSR (explained by the model) + SSE (residual error).   \(R^2 = SSR / SST\)

For an ordinary least-squares model with an intercept evaluated on the same data used to fit it, \(R^2\) ranges from \(0\) to \(1\). An \(R^2\) of \(0.85\) means the model explains 85% of the variance in \(Y\) relative to predicting the mean alone.

C. Adjusted \(R^2\): The Penalty Metric

Caution: The Flaw of \(R^2\)
Every time you add a predictor to a nested OLS model, even pure noise, \(R^2\) will increase or stay the same. It cannot decrease, which encourages overfitting.

Adjusted \(R^2\) penalizes extra predictors based on sample size \(n\) and number of predictors \(p\):

$$\text{Adjusted }R^2 = 1 - \left[\frac{(1 - R^2)(n - 1)}{n - p - 1}\right]$$
  • Adjusted \(R^2\) increases only if a new predictor improves the model more than expected by chance.
  • Adjusted \(R^2\) decreases if a weak or useless predictor is added.
  • When comparing MLR models with different numbers of predictors, use Adjusted \(R^2\).

Quick Reference

MetricRangeBehavior When Adding VariablesBest Used For
SSE / RSS\(\ge 0\)Always decreases or stays the same for nested OLS modelsOLS optimization; comparing models on the same outcome scale
\(R^2\)Usually \([0, 1]\) for in-sample OLS with an interceptAlways increases or stays the same for nested OLS modelsDescribing explained variance, especially in simple regression

8 Beyond MSE: Robust Regression

Robust Loss Robust loss functions Click to expand.

OLS regression is mathematically elegant, but it can be fragile when data contain outliers. In clinical, biomechanical, and physiological research, extreme observations deserve investigation rather than automatic deletion. Equipment malfunctions, subject non-compliance, movement artifacts, and biological extremes can all produce data points that strongly influence an OLS fit.

Why L2 Breaks Under Outliers

OLS minimizes the sum of squared residuals. Squaring is the source of the problem: a residual of 10 contributes 100 to the loss, ten times more than ten residuals of 1 would. A single outlier with a large residual can dominate the optimization, pulling the regression line toward itself and away from the bulk of the data.

$$\text{L2 Loss:}\quad \rho(e) = e^2 \qquad\implies\qquad \text{influence grows quadratically as } |e| \to \infty$$
Physiological Context - When Outliers Are Inevitable
  • VO2max testing: A subject who terminated early (pain/nausea) gives a spuriously low value, biasing the group regression.
  • Force plate data: Signal dropout or vibration artifacts produce extreme force spikes that dwarf the true biomechanical signal.
  • Wearable sensors: Poor electrode contact generates physiologically impossible heart-rate or EMG readings.
  • Clinical cohorts: Transcription errors, calibration drift, or subject non-compliance during a measurement window.

The Loss Function Landscape

A loss function \(\rho(e)\) determines how harshly each residual \(e = Y_i - \hat{Y}_i\) is penalized. The choice of loss defines the regression estimator.

Standard
L2 - Ordinary Least Squares
\(\rho(e) = e^2\)
Penalty grows quadratically. OLS is efficient when model assumptions are reasonable and residuals are approximately normal, but it is sensitive to outliers. A single extreme point can strongly affect the fitted line.
Robust
L1 - Least Absolute Deviations
\(\rho(e) = |e|\)
Penalty grows linearly, so large vertical residuals receive less extra influence than they do under L2. LAD estimates a conditional median relationship rather than a conditional mean. Limitation: non-differentiable at zero, less efficient than L2 for clean normal data, and still vulnerable to high-leverage points in \(X\).
Hybrid
Huber Loss
\(\rho(e;\delta) = \begin{cases} \tfrac{1}{2}e^2 & |e|\le\delta \\ \delta|e|-\tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2 & |e|>\delta \end{cases}\)
Most commonly used robust loss function. Quadratic near zero and linear for large residuals, so it behaves like OLS for small errors while reducing the influence of large residuals. The threshold \(\delta\) is a tuning constant, often chosen with a robust residual scale estimate such as the median absolute deviation (MAD).
Figure - Loss Function Shapes  (\(\delta = 2\) for Huber)
L2 (OLS) - quadratic, unbounded influence L1 (LAD) - linear, reduced influence from large residuals Huber - hybrid, smooth transition at \(\delta\)

M-estimators: IRLS

Robust regression is often solved with Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS), which converts the robust problem into a sequence of weighted OLS problems:

1
Initialize
Begin with the OLS solution \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}\) as the starting estimate.
2
Compute Weights
Get residuals \(e_i\). Estimate residual scale, often with \(s = \text{MAD}/0.6745\). Set \(w_i = 1\) if \(|e_i/s| \le \delta\), else \(w_i = \delta / |e_i/s|\). Large residuals get smaller \(w_i\).
3
Weighted OLS
Solve \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{W}\mathbf{y}\) - a standard weighted regression with the current weights.
4
Iterate to Convergence
Repeat steps 2-3. Each iteration, observations with large standardized residuals receive smaller weights; observations with small residuals retain weight 1. Typically converges in a small number of iterations.

Weighted Fit Summary

Standard \(R^2\) is built on squared deviations and the arithmetic mean, so it can be sensitive to outliers. A single extreme point can inflate SST and reduce \(R^2\), even when the model fits the bulk of the data well.

One useful descriptive summary for a robust fit is a weighted pseudo-\(R^2\) using the final IRLS weights \(w_i\):

$$R^2_{\text{robust}} = 1 - \frac{\displaystyle\sum_i w_i (Y_i - \hat{Y}_i)^2}{\displaystyle\sum_i w_i (Y_i - \bar{Y}_w)^2} \qquad\text{where}\quad \bar{Y}_w = \frac{\sum_i w_i Y_i}{\sum_i w_i}$$

Observations with low \(w_i\) contribute little to either the numerator or denominator, so this weighted summary reflects fit mainly for the observations that the robust procedure treated as more reliable. It is a descriptive pseudo-\(R^2\), not a universally standardized replacement for ordinary \(R^2\).

💡 Conceptual Key
Robust regression assigns observations with large standardized residuals reduced influence. The IRLS reweighting mechanism ensures that extreme residuals receive smaller weights, while observations close to the fitted pattern retain weight 1. The result is often a more stable estimate, but influential high-leverage points and data-entry errors still need diagnostic checking.
